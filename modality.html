Modes of communication

Modality

Korean cigarette boxes mostly use the visual, linguistic, and spatial modes. The interesting part is that the government and the tobacco companies use the same modes, and even the same design principles, for completely opposite goals.

Visual Mode

The warning image is the first thing you see because it has the most emphasis on the box. Pictures like open-heart surgery or a baby being fed cigarette butts take over the front, and you don't need to read Korean to get the message. Lee et al. (2020) found that smokers who felt stronger negative emotions from these images were more motivated to quit.

The brand side uses contrast to compete. Marlboro's bright green and yellow clash with the dark warning photo, and KT&G's white box and soft orange circle feel calm next to the surgery image. The four matching icons at the bottom also use repetition, so the scent capsule's benefits are quick to read.

Linguistic Mode

The government's warning text stands out with bold black letters on a bright yellow panel. It's chunked into a few short lines covering the age limit (19), the carcinogens in the smoke, and the quitline number. Every image also has a caption that starts with "The road to…," and that repetition makes the warnings easy to recognize.

Brands have to be sneakier with their words. Flavor names are banned, so they use names like "garden splash" or "Black Fresh" to hint at a taste (Heo et al., 2026). Marlboro's fine print backs up its "Smoke Smell Control" claim with a survey, but it's printed so small that it doesn't take away from the logo.

Spatial Mode

Proportion is a big deal here. The law says the warning has to cover the top half of the front, so the box is split right down the middle. Each half gets the same amount of space, but they pull your eye in opposite directions.

On the bottom half, Marlboro keeps things clean with a centered logo, icons lined up in a row, and some whitespace around them. Placing the badge, icons, and fine print close together (proximity) makes all the selling points feel like one message.

The box's location matters too. Packs sit behind the counter, so every customer has to face them while paying. Screven (1999) says public messages usually rely on people choosing to pay attention, but here the warnings are hard to avoid.

How the Modes Overlap

Each warning image comes with a "The road to…" caption, so the picture and the words deliver the same message together. On the brand side, Marlboro pairs green and yellow with the name "garden splash," so the color and the wording both hint at a fresh flavor. The icons at the bottom also have short Korean labels underneath them. The spatial mode holds everything together, since the 50/50 layout decides where each image and line of text goes.
