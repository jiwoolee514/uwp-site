Modes of communication

Modality

A cigarette box gets its message across in seconds, often without anyone reading a word. Here's how it uses each of the five modes.

Primary mode: visual. Both authors depend on images. The government uses a shocking photo because you can't miss it at a glance, and the brand uses color and design because words like "light" are no longer allowed.

The five modes

The box mixes several modes, and each author uses them differently.

Aa

Linguistic

Two languages, two voices. The warnings are blunt, official Korean sentences. Brand names are short English or European-style words that sound stylish. The side-panel chemical list sounds clinical and scientific.

◐

Visual

Graphic, realistic photos of disease set against sleek, minimal brand design. Color does a lot of the work: soft blues and silvers suggest smoothness, and the dark, bloody tones of the warning photos suggest danger.

♪

Aural

Mostly not used. Small sounds do become part of the experience, though: the crinkle of the cellophane, the snap of the flip-top lid, and the "click" of crushing a menthol capsule.

▦

Spatial

The layout is a split screen. Warnings take the top, where the eye lands first and where they stay visible on a shelf. Branding gets the bottom and the slim side panels. The small, pocket-sized box keeps it close to the body all day.

✋

Gestural

The box is handled over and over: flipping the lid, pulling out a cigarette, offering one to a friend. Many smokers also slip packs into cases or turn them face-down to hide the warning, a gesture that pushes back against the genre itself.

How the modes work together

On a Korean cigarette box, the modes compete instead of cooperating. The warning combines a disturbing photo, plain words and the top spot to interrupt the habit. The brand combines attractive color, a stylish name and a pleasing shape and feel to protect it.

The genre exists because neither side can fully win, and every time someone picks up a pack, they experience both messages at once.
